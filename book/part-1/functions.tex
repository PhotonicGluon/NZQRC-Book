\Chapter{Functions}
\Lettrine[loversize=0.1,nindent=0pt]{Picture} a machine that takes in inputs and produces outputs. It could, for instance, double every number put into it. It may return the sum of all of its inputs, the product of all its inputs, or the largest number among all the inputs. That is what a function is -- a relation that maps inputs to outputs. It is this kind of particular relation that we look at in this chapter.

\section{What is a Function?}
\begin{definition}[Function]\label{definition:function}
    A \term{function}\index{function} $f$ from a set $X$ to a set $Y$ is a relation from $X$ to $Y$ satisfying two properties.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{F\arabic*.},leftmargin=1.35\parindent]
        \item $\forall x \in X, \exists y \in Y \text{ s.t. } (x \mathrel{f} y)$
        \item $\forall x \in X, \forall y_1, y_2 \in Y, (((x \mathrel{f} y_1) \land (x \mathrel{f} y_2)) \implies (y_1 = y_2))$
    \end{enumerate}
    We denote such a function by $f: X \to Y$.
\end{definition}

The condition \textbf{F2} essentially says that if a single $x \in X$ is related to two (or more) elements in $Y$, then they must be equal. This can be thought of as the ``uniqueness'' property of a function.

We can succinctly write both \textbf{F1} and \textbf{F2} in one statement using the \term{uniqueness quantifier}\index{uniqueness quantifier}\index{quantifier!uniqueness} $\exists!$. A statement of the form $\exists! x \text{ s.t. } P(x)$, where $P(x)$ is a predicate, is logically equivalent to the statement
\[
    \exists x \text{ s.t. } (P(x) \land (\forall y (P(y) \implies x = y))),
\]
i.e. there exists an $x$ that satisfies the predicate and any $y$ satisfying the predicate must be equal to $x$. Thus, we can combine \textbf{F1} and \textbf{F2} into the single condition
\[
    \forall x \in X, \exists! y \in Y \text{ s.t. } x \mathrel{f} y.
\]
This formulation of the function condition is what we will be using most of the time.

As mentioned, we may think of a function as a machine or process that takes in inputs and produce outputs. In particular, the `machine' for the function $f: X \to Y$ is a machine that \textbf{must} produce an output for any valid input (i.e., an input that comes from $X$) that is provided to it, and it must produce the same output for the same input that is provided to it.

Since there is only one output for any given input, we can denote it unambiguously.

\begin{definition}[Image of an Element Under a Function]\label{definition:image-of-element-under-a-function}
    Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets and $f: X \to Y$ be a function. Suppose $x \in X$. The unique element of $Y$ that is related to $x$ under $f$ is called the \term{image of $x$ under $f$}\index{image!of an element}\index{function!image!of an element} and is denoted $f(x)$.
\end{definition}

Let's now turn our attention to determining the equality of two functions. Recall that two relations $R$ and $S$ from the set $A$ to the set $B$ and the set $C$ to the set $D$ respectively (i.e., $R \subseteq A \times B$ and $S \subseteq C \times D$) are equal if and only if $A = C$, $B = D$, and $R = S$ as sets (\myref{definition:equality-of-relations}). Since functions are also relations, this definition applies to functions.

\begin{definition}[Equality of Functions]\index{function!equality}
    Let $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ be sets. The functions $f: A \to B$ and $g: C \to D$ are equal, denoted $f = g$, if and only if $A = C$, $B = D$, and $f = g$ as sets.
\end{definition}

However, we have a more applicable result for functions.
\begin{theorem}[Equality of Functions, More Easily]
    Let $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ be sets. Let the functions $f: A \to B$ and $g: C \to D$. Then $f = g$ if and only if $A = C$, $B = D$, and $f(x) = g(x)$ for all $x \in A$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    For the forward direction, suppose $f = g$ (as functions). Thus $A = C$, $B = D$, and $f = g$ as sets. Suppose $x \in A$ and $y \in B$ are arbitrary. Note
    \begin{align*}
        & y = f(x)\\
        \iff& (x, y) \in f & (\myref{definition:image-of-element-under-a-function})\\
        \iff& (x, y) \in g & (\text{as } f = g \text{ as sets})\\
        \iff& y = g(x) & (\myref{definition:image-of-element-under-a-function})
    \end{align*}
    which means $f(x) = g(x)$ for any $x \in A$.

    For the reverse direction, suppose $A = C$, $B = D$, and $f(x) = g(x)$ for all $x \in A$. Then
    \begin{align*}
        & (x, y) \in f\\
        \iff& y = f(x) & (\myref{definition:image-of-element-under-a-function})\\
        \iff& y = g(x) & (\text{as } f(x) = g(x)\;\forall x \in A)\\
        \iff& (x, y) \in g & (\myref{definition:image-of-element-under-a-function})
    \end{align*}
    which means that $f = g$ as sets. Thus $f = g$ by definition of equality of functions.
\end{proof}

There are three important sets related to a function.

\begin{definition}[Domain, Codomain, and Range (Functions)]
    Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets and let $f: X \to Y$ be a function.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The \term{domain}\index{domain}\index{relation!domain} of $f$ is the set $X$.
        \item The \term{codomain}\index{codomain}\index{relation!codomain} of $f$ is the set $Y$.
        \item The \term{range}\index{range}\index{relation!range} of $f$ is the set $\{y \in Y \vert \exists x \in X \text{ s.t. } y = f(x)\}$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}[Image of a Function]
    Some authors may call the range of a function the \term{image}\index{image}\index{image!of a function}\index{function!image}\index{function!image!of a function} of the function. However, this would likely lead to confusion with the image of an element under a function, so we will stick with ``range''.
\end{remark}

\section{Axiom Schema of Replacement}
We now look at the axiom schema of replacement.

\subsection{The Need for the Axiom Schema}
We often define functions with a rule. For example, the successor function that is restricted to the natural numbers, i.e. $S: \N \to \N$, is defined by $S(n) = n \cup \{n\}$ for all $n \in \N$. Using relation notation, we can write this as
\[
    n \mathrel{S} m \iff m = n \cup \{n\}.
\]
Another example would be the "character of" function in \myref{example:character-of-function}, where we defined a function $f: N \to C$ (where $N = \{1, 2, 3\}$ and $C = \{+, \text{a}, \text{b}, \text{c}, \text{d}, \text{e}\}$) such that $f(1) = \text{a}$, $f(2) = \text{b}$, and $f(3) = \text{c}$. We can write this as
\[
    x \mathrel{f} y \iff (x = 1 \land y = \text{a}) \lor (x = 2 \land y = \text{b}) \lor (x = 3 \land y = \text{c}).
\]
What these two examples show is that a function $f: X \to Y$ can be defined as
\[
    x \mathrel{f} y \iff P(x, y)
\]
where $P(x,y)$ is a binary predicate.

Essentially, what we want to ensure is that every function's image is indeed a set. Unfortunately, we are unable to quantify over definable functions in our language of axiomatic set theory. So, we are stuck with using an axiom schema that should achieve the same effect.

\subsection{Forming the Axiom Schema}
Let's start with our goal, expressed in a high-level manner: for every function, its range exists. As explained earlier, we are not able to quantify over functions, but we are able to quantify over \wffs~(which include predicates).

Suppose we have a function $f: X \to Y$ such that
\[
    x \mathrel{f} y \iff \phi(x, y, X)
\]
where $\phi$ is a \wff. In essence, the \wff~$\phi$ is the \term{rule}\index{function!rule} of $f$ -- it defines which elements in $Y$ could be an image of an element from $X$. Note that $X$ needs to be a free variable in $\phi$ because the function $f$ may use it to determine which elements are within its range. Now recall that $f$ is a function if and only if
\[
    \forall x \in X, \exists! y \in Y \text{ s.t. } x \mathrel{f} y.
\]
Thus
\[
    \forall x \in X, \exists! y \in Y \text{ s.t. } \phi(x, y, X)
\]
is the condition that must be satisfied for $f$ to be a function. In other words, using semi-formal language, we can write that condition above as
\[
    \forall x (x \in X \implies \exists! y (y \in Y \land \phi(x, y, X))).
\]
Now, for our purposes, we actually want to make the condition a little weaker by removing the need for $y$ to come from a known set $Y$. This is because the axiom itself will bring into existence a set that contains the images of all the elements in $X$, so $y$ just needs to exist. Thus, the antecedent for our condition in the axiom schema of replacement is
\[
    \forall x (x \in X \implies \exists! y \phi(x, y, X)).
\]

We turn our attention to the consequent of the axiom schema of replacement -- that the range of the function $f$ exists. In other words, there must exist a set containing all the images of the elements in $X$, and that any image of an element in $X$ must be in that set. Note that if $y$ is an image of an element in $X$, that means that there exists an $x \in X$ such that $y = f(x)$ (i.e., $x \mathrel{f} y$, or $\phi(x, y, X)$ is true). We can express this idea as
\[
    \exists R \text{ s.t. } \forall y (y \in R \iff \exists x \in X \text{ s.t. } \phi(x, y, X))
\]
where one can interpret $R$ as the range of $f$. Formally, we can write this as
\[
    \exists R \forall y (y \in R \iff \exists x (x \in X \land \phi(x, y, X))).
\]

There are a few more tweaks needed before we can finally arrive at the axiom schema of replacement. First, let us allow $\phi$ to have other free variables other than just $x$, $y$ and $X$. In particular, let $w_1$, $w_2$, \dots, $w_n$ be free variables in $\phi$. So we can write the antecedent and consequent of the axiom schema of replacement semi-formally as
\[
    \forall x (x \in X \implies \exists! y \phi(x, y, w_1, \dots, w_n, X))
\]
and
\[
    \exists R \forall y (y \in R \iff \exists x (x \in X \land \phi(x, y, w_1, \dots, w_n, X)))
\]
for all $X$, $w_1$, $w_2$, \dots, $w_n$. We then combine them into one statement, resulting in the semi-formal statement
\begin{align*}
    &\forall X \forall w_1 \forall w_2 \cdots \forall w_n (\\
    &\quad \forall x (x \in X \implies \exists! y \phi(x, y, w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n, X))\\
    &\hquad\implies \exists R \forall y (y \in R \iff \exists x (x \in X \land \phi(x, y, w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n, X)))\\
    &).
\end{align*}
Lastly, recalling that the uniqueness quantifier is defined such that
\[
    \exists! x \psi(x) \equiv \exists x (\psi(x) \land \forall y (\psi(y) \implies y = x)),
\]
we can finally write state the axiom schema of replacement formally.

\begin{axiom}[Axiom Schema of Replacement]\label{axiom:schema-of-replacement}\index{axiom!schema of replacement}\index{Zermelo-Frankel Set Theory!schema of replacement}
    Let $\phi$ be a \wff. Then
    \begin{align*}
        &\forall X \forall w_1 \forall w_2 \cdots \forall w_n (\\
        &\quad \forall x (x \in X \implies \exists y (\\
        &\quad\quad \phi(x, y, w_1, \dots, w_n, X)\\
        &\quad\quad\land \forall z (\phi(x, z, w_1, \dots, w_n, X) \implies z = y)\\
        &\quad\hquad)\\
        &\quad)\implies \exists R \forall y (y \in R \iff \exists x (x \in X \land \phi(x, y, w_1, \dots, w_n, X)))\\
        &)
    \end{align*}
    where $R$ is not free in $\phi$.
\end{axiom}

This is undoubtedly one of the most involved axiom schema within \ZF, but also one of the most powerful. As we will soon see, functions play a crucial part when working with numbers.

\section{Well-Defined Functions}
\myref{axiom:schema-of-replacement} allows us to assert that the range of any function exists. However, care must be taken to ensure that the rule of the function indeed makes the function a function, and not just a relation. Specifically, referring to \myref{definition:function}, a `function' that fails to satisfy either \textbf{F1} or \textbf{F2} (or both) is called a \term{ill-defined} function, and functions that satisfy both \textbf{F1} and \textbf{F2} are called \term{well-defined} functions (which are actual functions).

\begin{example}[A Possibly Ill-Defined Function]
    Let $S_1$ and $S_2$ be non-empty sets, and let $S = S_1 \cup S_2$. Let $f$ be a relation from $S$ to $\{1,2\}$ such that
    \[
        f = \{(s, 1) \vert s \in S_1\} \cup \{(s, 2) \vert s \in S_2\}.
    \]
    In other words,
    \[
        x \mathrel{f} y \iff (x \in S_1 \land y = 1) \lor (x \in S_2 \land y = 2).
    \]
    Is this a (well-defined) function?

    Suppose $S_1 \cap S_2 = \emptyset$. This means that any element in $S_1$ would have an image of 1, and any element in $S_2$ would have an image of 2. In particular, every element from $S$ will have one and only one image. Thus $f$ is indeed a function. In fact, we can write it using a \term{piecewise definition}\index{piecewise definition}\index{function!piecewise definition} as
    \[
        f(x) = \begin{cases}
            1 & \text{if } x \in S_1\\
            2 & \text{if } x \in S_2
        \end{cases}
    \]

    However, what happens when $S_1 \cap S_2 \neq \emptyset$? Suppose $S_1 = \{1,2\}$ and $S_2=\{2, 3\}$. Then we see
    \[
        f = \{(1, 1), (2, 1), (2, 2), (3, 2)\}.
    \]
    In particular, we see that $2\mathrel{f}1$ and $2\mathrel{f}2$ simultaneously. If we were to abuse some notation, this means that $f(2) = 1$ and $f(2) = 2$ simultaneously. This clearly violates \textbf{F2} in \myref{definition:function}, and thus $f$ is not a (well-defined) function in this case (i.e., $f$ is ill-defined as a function).

    In conclusion, for $f: S \to \{1,2\}$ to be a function such that
    \[
        f(x) = \begin{cases}
            1 & \text{if } x \in S_1\\
            2 & \text{if } x \in S_2
        \end{cases}
    \]
    we must have $S_1 \cap S_2 = \emptyset$.
\end{example}

        \item The relation $h$ from $\N$ to $\N^2$ such that

\section{Injections, Surjections, Bijections}
We now look at two essential properties that functions could satisfy -- injectivity and surjectivity. Bijectivity is just the combination of both injectivity and surjectivity, and it results in the elements of the domain and codomain matching up exactly. Such a bijective function can result in a new function that undoes the original function, called the inverse function.

\subsection{Injectivity}
A function could send multiple elements in the domain to the same element in the codomain. The function's arrow diagram would have two arrows originating at different elements and terminating at the same element. Such a function is \term{non-injective}. It would be nice for a function to have no two distinct elements in the domain map to the same element in the codomain. Diagrammatically, this means that no two arrows originating at different elements terminate at the same element. For example, the ``character of'' function in \myref{example:character-of-function} satisfies this property. Such a function is \term{injective}.

We can express the condition for a function to be injective formally.
\begin{definition}[Injectivity]
    Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets, and let $f: X \to Y$ be a function. Then $f$ is \term{injective}\index{injective}\index{function!injective} (or \term{one-to-one}\index{function!one-to-one}) if and only if
    \[
        f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2
    \]
    for all $x_1, x_2 \in X$. Equivalently,
    \[
        x_1 \neq x_2 \implies f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)
    \]
    for all $x_1, x_2 \in X$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[An Example of Injectivity]
    (\textit{Assume the addition and multiplication of natural numbers is defined.})

    Consider the function $f: \N \to \N$ such that $f(x) = 4x + 1$ for all $x \in \N$. We show that this is injective.
    \begin{proof}
        Suppose $x_1, x_2 \in \N$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. By definition of $f$, we have $4x_1 + 1 = 4x_2 + 1$. So $4x_1 = 4x_2$ and thus $x_1 = x_2$. So $f$ is indeed injective by definition.
    \end{proof}
\end{example}

\begin{example}[An Example of Non-Injectivity]
    Consider the function $f: \N \to \N$ such that $f(x) =(\text{last digit of } x)$. We show that this is not injective.
    \begin{proof}
        Note $1 \in \N$ and $11 \in \N$. We see $f(1) = f(11) = 1$, so $f$ is not injective.
    \end{proof}
\end{example}

\subsection{Surjectivity}
Recall that a relation from the set $X$ to the set $Y$ may not have its domain being $X$, but a function has to have its domain to be the entire set $Y$. Likewise, a function's range may not be the whole set $Y$, but it would be nice to have such a function with its range as the codomain. This is the appeal of \term{surjective} functions.

\begin{definition}[Surjectivity]
    Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets, and let $f: X \to Y$ be a function. Then $f$ is \term{surjective}\index{surjective}\index{function!surjective} (or \term{onto}\index{function!onto}) if and only if
    \[
        \forall y \in Y, \exists x \in X \text{ s.t. } y = f(x).
    \]
    In other words, every $y \in Y$ has a corresponding $x \in X$, called its \term{preimage}\index{preimage}\index{function!preimage}, such that $y = f(x)$.
\end{definition}

As mentioned, an important property of surjective functions is that their range is their codomain.

\begin{proposition}[Surjective Functions' Range is their Codomain]
    Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets, and let $f: X \to Y$ be a surjective function. Then the range of $f$ is $Y$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Recall that the range of $f$ is the set
    \[
        \{y \in Y \vert \exists x \in X \text{ s.t. } y = f(x)\}.
    \]
    Since $f$ is surjective, every $y \in Y$ has a corresponding preimage $x \in X$ such that $y = f(x)$. Thus the range of $f$ is exactly $Y$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Bijectivity}
Now, let us consider a function $f: X \to Y$ which is both injective and bijective. Such functions are so important that we have a name for them -- \term{bijections}.

\begin{definition}[Bijectivity]
    Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets, and let $f: X \to Y$ be a function. Then $f$ is \term{bijective}\index{bijective}\index{function!bijective} (or that $f$ is a \term{one-to-one correspondence}\index{function!one-to-one correspondence}) if and only if it is both injective and surjective.
\end{definition}

Why are they important? For a bijection $f: X \to Y$, any $x \in X$ has a unique corresponding element $y \in Y$ such that $y = f(x)$ (since $f$ is a function). Also, any $y \in Y$ has a corresponding preimage $x \in X$ such that $y = f(x)$ (since $f$ is surjective) and this $x$ is that only one that results in $y$ (since $f$ is injective). In essence, a bijection creates a pairing between the elements of $X$ and $Y$ that matches each element of $X$ exactly one element of $Y$ (and vice versa).

\begin{example}[The ``Decimal to Binary'' Function]\label{example:decimal-to-binary-function}
    A binary string consists of only ones and zeros. Let $N$, a subset of all natural numbers written in decimal (i.e., base 10), be $\{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$. Let $B$, a subset of all binary strings, be $\{\code{000}, \code{001}, \code{010}, \code{011}, \code{100}, \code{101}\}$. We may define the function $f: N \to B$ such that, in effect, it converts the decimal number in $N$ into a binary string in $B$. That is,
    \[
        f = \{(0, \code{000}), (1, \code{001}), (2, \code{010}), (3, \code{011}), (4, \code{100}), (5, \code{101})\}.
    \]
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[height=3cm]{images/part-1/functions/decimal-to-binary.png}
        \caption{Arrow Diagram of $f$}
    \end{figure}
    We clearly see from the arrow diagram that
    \begin{itemize}
        \item each element of $B$ has no more than one arrow pointing to it, which means $f$ is injective; and
        \item every element of $B$ has an arrow pointing to it, which means $f$ is surjective,
    \end{itemize}
    and thus $f$ is bijective (which means it is a bijection).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The String Reversal Function]\label{example:string-reversal-function}
    Suppose $\Lang$ is a formal language with alphabet $\Alphabet$. Let $\Alphabet^\ast$ denote the set of all strings from using symbols from $\Alphabet$. Define the function $r: \Alphabet^\ast \to \Alphabet^\ast$ where, for any string $s \in \Alphabet^\ast$, $r(s)$ is the string obtained by reversing $s$. For example, for $\Alphabet = \{a,b,c\}$,
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $r(\emptystring) = \emptystring$ (since we do not have any symbols to reverse);
        \item $r(a) = a$, and likewise $r(b) = b$ and $r(c) = c$;
        \item $r(ab) = ba$, and likewise $r(ac) = ca$ and $r(bc) = cb$;
        \item $r(abcaba) = abacba$; and
        \item $r(cacab) = bacac$.
    \end{itemize}

    We show that $r$ is actually a bijection.
    \begin{proof}
        We are to show two things: $r$ is injective and $r$ is surjective.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Injective}: Suppose $s_1, s_2 \in \Alphabet^\ast$ such that $r(s_1) = r(s_2)$. What does this mean? This means that the string obtained when writing the symbols in $s_1$ in reverse order is the same as the string obtained when writing the symbols in $s_2$ in reverse order. But if $s_1$ and $s_2$ are the same when written in reverse, they have to be the same to start with. In other words, we must have $s_1 = s_2$ and thus $r$ is indeed injective.
            \item \textbf{Surjective}: Suppose $s'$ is any string in $\Alphabet^\ast$. By definition of $r$, we know that $r(s')$ is the string obtained by reversing $s'$. Note that when a reversed string is reversed again, we obtain the original string, i.e. $r(r(s')) = s'$. So let $s = r(s')$, noting that $s \in \Alphabet^\ast$. Then $r(s) = r(r(s')) = s'$, and thus $r$ is indeed surjective.
        \end{itemize}
        Since $r$ is both injective and surjective, it is thus bijective.
    \end{proof}
\end{example}

Bijections create a function that undoes the effect of the original function. This function is called the \term{inverse function}, and only bijections have them.

\begin{definition}[Invertible Function and Inverse Function]
    Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets, and let $f: X \to Y$ be a function. Then $f$ is an \term{invertible function}\index{function!invertible} if and only if there exists a function $g: Y \to X$ such that
    \begin{align*}
        g(f(x)) &= x \text{ for all } x \in X \text{ and}\\
        f(g(y)) &= y \text{ for all } y \in Y.
    \end{align*}
    In this case $g$ is called the \term{inverse function of $f$}\index{function!inverse}, and is usually denoted $f^{-1}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Invertible If and Only If Bijective]\label{theorem:invertible-if-and-only-if-bijective}
    Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets, and let $f: X \to Y$ be a function. Then $f$ is invertible if and only if $f$ is a bijection.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    For the forward direction, suppose $f$ is invertible. This means there exists $g: Y \to X$ such that $g(f(x)) = x$ for all $x \in X$ and $f(g(y)) = y$ for all $y \in Y$. We are to prove that $f$ is both injective and surjective.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Injective}: Suppose $x_1, x_2 \in X$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Thus $g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$, which means $x_1 = x_2$ by definition of $g$.
        \item \textbf{Surjective}: Suppose $y \in Y$. We know that $g(y) \in X$ by definition of $g$. Furthermore we know that $f(g(y)) = y$ by definition of $g$. So the preimage of $y$ under $f$ is $g(y)$, which shows that $f$ is surjective.
    \end{itemize}
    As $f$ is both injective and surjective, it is thus bijective.

    For the reverse direction, let $f: X \to Y$ be a bijection. Since a function is a relation, thus $f$ is a subset of $X \times Y$. Considering $f$ as a relation, let $g = f^{-1}$, i.e. $g$ is the inverse relation of $f$. We show that $g$ is the inverse function of $f$.

    First, to prove that $g = f^{-1}$ is indeed a function. We need to prove \textbf{F1} and \textbf{F2} (see \myref{definition:function}). In the context of $g$, they can be written as follows.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{F\arabic*.}]
        \item For all $y \in Y$, there exists $x \in X$ such that $y \mathrel{g} x$.
        \item For all $y \in Y$, if $y \mathrel{g} x_1$ and $y \mathrel{g} x_2$ for some $x_1, x_2 \in X$, we must have $x_1 = x_2$.
    \end{enumerate}
    We prove them one by one, using the properties of $f$.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item To prove \textbf{F1}, suppose $y \in Y$. Since $f$ is surjective, we know that there exists a $x \in X$ such that $f(x) = y$ (i.e. $x \mathrel{f} y$). In particular we know $y \mathrel{f^{-1}} x$ by definition of inverse relation; since $g = f^{-1}$ we must have $y \mathrel{g} x$. So there is an $x \in X$ such that $y \mathrel{g} x$ (and in particular it is the $x \in X$ such that $f(x) = y$).
        \item To prove \textbf{F2}, suppose $y \in Y$ and $x_1, x_2 \in X$ such that $y \mathrel{g} x_1$ and $y \mathrel{g} x_2$. By our working above, we know that $x_1 \mathrel{f} y$ and $x_2 \mathrel{f} y$, i.e. $f(x_1) = y$ and $f(x_2) = y$. Thus $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Since $f$ is injective, thus $x_1 = x_2$.
    \end{itemize}
    Thus $g = f^{-1}$ is a function.

    Now we need to prove that $g(f(x)) = x$ for all $x \in X$. As above we know there exists a $y \in Y$ such that $x \mathrel{f} y$ and $y \mathrel{g} x$. Clearly this means $g(f(x)) = x$ for all $x \in X$.

    Finally, we need to prove that $f(g(y)) = y$ for all $y \in Y$. As above we know there exists a $x \in X$ such that $y \mathrel{g} x$ and $x \mathrel{f} y$. Clearly this means $f(g(y)) = y$ for all $y \in Y$.

    Hence we see $f$ is invertible with $g$ being the inverse function of $f$.
\end{proof}

What the above theorem states is that the inverse function of $f: X \to Y$ is exactly the function induced by the inverse relation of $f$.

        f = \{(0, \code{000}), (1, \code{001}), (2, \code{010}), (3, \code{011}), (4, \code{100}), (5, \code{101})\}.
    \]
    So the inverse function of $f$ is
    \[
        f^{-1} = \{(\code{000}, 0), (\code{001}, 1), (\code{010}, 2), (\code{011}, 3), (\code{100}, 4), (\code{101}, 5)\}.

\section{Composition of Functions}
We may think of function composition as machines feeding into each other -- the output of one machine is the input of the other.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[height=2cm]{images/part-1/functions/function-composition_machine.png}
    \caption{Composition of Functions $f$ and $g$}
\end{figure}
In the above figure, we have two functions, namely $f$ and $g$. Any input $x$ that is fed into this machine will first pass through $f$, resulting in $f(x)$, and then pass through $g$, with the result being $g(f(x))$.

\begin{definition}[Composition of Functions]
    Let $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ be sets. Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: C \to D$ be functions. The \term{composition of $f$ and $g$}\index{function!composition}, denoted $g \circ f$, is the function $h: A \to D$ such that
    \[
        h(x) = g(f(x))
    \]
    for all $x \in A$, and is only defined if and only if $C$ is a subset of the range of $f$ (i.e., the domain of $g$ is a subset of the range of $f$).
\end{definition}

This definition can be displayed diagrammatically.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[height=4.5cm]{images/part-1/functions/function-composition_arrow-diagram.png}
    \caption{Composition of Functions $f$ and $g$}
\end{figure}
In the diagram, any element in the domain of $g\circ f$ (i.e., any element in $A$) will first be mapped to the range of $f$ (denoted by the dotted circle within the intersection of $B$ and $C$), and then mapped to the final element in $D$ (which is the codomain of $g \circ f$).

One might wonder whether the order of taking the compositions matter. It turns out that it does not.

\begin{theorem}[Associativity of Composition (Functions)]\index{function!composition!associativity}
    Let $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, $E$, and $F$ be sets. Let $f: A \to B$, $g: C \to D$, and $h: E \to F$ be functions such that $g \circ f$ and $h \circ g$ are defined. Then
    \[
        (h \circ g) \circ f = h \circ (g \circ f).   
    \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Note that if either $f$, $g$, or $h$ is the empty function (see \myref{exercise:empty-function}), then $(h \circ g) \circ f = h \circ (g \circ f) = \emptyset$. So we may assume that $f$, $g$, and $h$ are not empty functions.

    Considering $f$ and $g$ as relations for a moment, the fact that $g \circ f$ is a function means that $C$ is a subset of the range of $f$ (which is a subset of $B$). Since $f$ and $g$ are not empty functions, thus we must have $B \cap C \neq \emptyset$. Likewise we see $D \cap E \neq \emptyset$ by using the same argument for $g$ and $h$.

    Therefore the claim is readily proven by using \myref{theorem:associativity-of-composition-relations} on $f$, $g$, and $h$ when we consider them as relations.
\end{proof}

Similar to relations, since there is no distinction between $(h \circ g) \circ f$ and $h \circ (g \circ f$), we can write ``$h \circ g \circ f$'' without any ambiguity.

The most trivial composition that we can consider is a composition with the identity function.

\begin{proposition}[Composition with Identity Function]
    Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets, and let $f: X \to Y$ be a function. Then
    \[
        f\circ \id_X = f \text{ and } \id_Y \circ f = f.
    \]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Recall that $\id_A(a) = a$ for all sets $A$ and $a \in A$. So for any $x \in X$ we have
    \begin{align*}
        (f \circ \id_X)(x) &= f(\id_X(x)) & (\text{Definition of composition})\\
        &= f(x) & (\text{Definition of identity function})
    \end{align*}
    which means $f \circ \id_X = f$. Also, we have
    \begin{align*}
        (\id_Y \circ f)(x) &= \id_Y(f(x)) & (\text{Definition of composition})\\
        &= f(x) & (\text{Definition of identity function})
    \end{align*}
    for any $x \in X$, meaning that $\id_Y \circ f = f$.
\end{proof}

A more interesting result can be found when we compose an invertible function with its inverse.

\begin{example}[Composing an Invertible Function with Its Inverse]
    Let $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$, $B = \{x, y, z\}$, and the function $f: A \to B$ such that
    \[
        f = \{(1, z), (2, x), (3, y)\}.
    \]
    Clearly $f$ is a bijection, which means it has an inverse. In particular, its inverse is
    \[
        f^{-1} = \{(x, 2), (y, 3), (z, 1)\}.
    \]
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \begin{subfigure}{0.45\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=3.5cm]{images/part-1/functions/compose-function-with-inverse_f.png}
            \caption{Arrow Diagram of $f$}
        \end{subfigure}
        \hfill
        \begin{subfigure}{0.45\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=3.5cm]{images/part-1/functions/compose-function-with-inverse_f-inv.png}
            \caption{Arrow Diagram of $f^{-1}$}
        \end{subfigure}
        \caption{Arrow Diagrams of $f$ and $f^{-1}$}
    \end{figure}

    Let us consider the composition $f^{-1} \circ f$. It can be found by following the arrows from $A$ to $B$ via $f$ and back to $A$ via $f^{-1}$. So we see that
    \begin{align*}
        (f^{-1} \circ f)(1) &= (f^{-1}(f(1))) = f^{-1}(z) = 1,\\
        (f^{-1} \circ f)(2) &= (f^{-1}(f(2))) = f^{-1}(x) = 2, \text{ and}\\
        (f^{-1} \circ f)(3) &= (f^{-1}(f(3))) = f^{-1}(y) = 3.
    \end{align*}
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[height=3cm]{images/part-1/functions/compose-function-with-inverse_aba.png}
        \caption{Arrow Diagram of $f^{-1} \circ f$}
    \end{figure}

    We can do the same for the composition $f \circ f^{-1}$. It can be found by following the arrows from $B$ to $A$ via $f^{-1}$ and back to $B$ via $f$. Thus
    \begin{align*}
        (f \circ f^{-1})(x) &= (f(f^{-1}(x))) = f(2) = x,\\
        (f \circ f^{-1})(y) &= (f(f^{-1}(y))) = f(3) = y, \text{ and}\\
        (f \circ f^{-1})(z) &= (f(f^{-1}(z))) = f(1) = z.
    \end{align*}
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[height=3cm]{images/part-1/functions/compose-function-with-inverse_bab.png}
        \caption{Arrow Diagram of $f \circ f^{-1}$}
    \end{figure}

    One sees from the above two computations that
    \[
        f^{-1} \circ f = \id_A \text{ and } f \circ f^{-1} = \id_B.
    \]
\end{example}

The observation made above can be generalised.

\begin{proposition}[Composition of Function with Inverse]
    Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets, and let $f: X \to Y$ be an invertible function with inverse $f^{-1}: Y \to X$. Then
    \[
        f^{-1} \circ f = \id_X \text{ and } f \circ f^{-1} = \id_Y.
    \]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    For the first, let $x \in X$. Then
    \[
        (f^{-1} \circ f)(x) = f^{-1}(f(x))
    \]
    by definition of composition of functions. Letting $z = f^{-1}(f(x))$, we see that
    \[
        f(z) = f(x)
    \]
    by definition of the inverse function. As $f$ is invertible, it is thus a bijection (\myref{theorem:invertible-if-and-only-if-bijective}), which means it is injective (by definition of bijection). Hence we see $z = x$, meaning $f^{-1}(f(x)) = x$. Since this holds for any $x \in X$, we must have $f^{-1} \circ f = \id_X$.

    For the second, let $y \in Y$. Then we see
    \[
        (f \circ f^{-1})(y) = f(f^{-1}(y)).
    \]
    Let $z = f(f^{-1}(y))$, which means that
    \[
        f^{-1}(z) = f^{-1}(y)
    \]
    by definition of the inverse function. Now note that $f^{-1}$ clearly has an inverse of $f$, which thus means that $f^{-1}$ is bijective (\myref{theorem:invertible-if-and-only-if-bijective}). So we must have $z = y$, meaning $f(f^{-1}(y)) = y$ for all $y \in Y$. Hence $f \circ f^{-1} = \id_Y$.
\end{proof}

It is not a coincidence that the composition of injective functions is also injective.

\begin{proposition}[Injective Function Composition is Injective]\label{prop:composition-of-injective-functions-is-injective}
    Let $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ be sets. Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: C \to D$ be injections such that $g \circ f$ is defined. Then $g \circ f$ is an injection.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Suppose $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$, i.e.
    \[
        g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))
    \]
    by definition of composition of functions. Since $g$ is injective, thus
    \[
        f(x_1) = f(x_2).
    \]
    But as $f$ is also injective, thus $x_1 = x_2$. Therefore $g \circ f$ is an injection.
\end{proof}

\begin{exercise}[Composition of an Injection and Non-Injection]
    Let $X$ be a non-empty set, and let $f: X \to X$, $g: X \to X$, and $h: X \to X$ be functions. Suppose $h$ is an injective function. Which of the following statements are always true?
    \begin{partquestions}{\alph*}
        \item If $h \circ f = h \circ g$ then $f = g$.
        \item If $f \circ h = g \circ h$ then $f = g$.\newline
        (\textit{Hint: think of infinite sets. You may use concepts that are not yet explicitly defined in this book, e.g. odd and even numbers, and multiplication of natural numbers.})
    \end{partquestions}
\end{exercise}

One may notice a similar phenomenon occurring with the composition of surjective functions.

\begin{example}[Composition of Surjective Functions]
    Let $A = \{a,b,c,d,e\}$, $B = \{\text{I}, \text{II}, \text{III}, \text{IV}\}$, and $C = \{1, 2, 3\}$. Define the functions $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ such that
    \[
        f = \{(a, \text{II}), (b, \text{III}), (c, \text{I}), (d, \text{IV}), (e, \text{I})\}
    \]
    and
    \[
        g = \{(\text{I}, 2), (\text{II}, 1), (\text{III}, 1), (\text{IV}, 3)\}.
    \]
    We can draw their individual effects as follows.
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[height=3cm]{images/part-1/functions/compose-surjections_abc.png}
        \caption{Arrow Diagrams of $f$ and $g$, On The Same Diagram}
    \end{figure}

    The overall composition of $f$ and $g$ is
    \[
        g \circ f = \{(a, 1), (b, 1), (c, 2), (d, 3), (e, 2)\}
    \]
    which has an arrow diagram as follows.
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[height=3cm]{images/part-1/functions/compose-surjections_ac.png}
        \caption{Arrow Diagram of $g \circ f$}
    \end{figure}
    One easily verifies that $g \circ f$ is also surjective.
\end{example}

Again, this is not a coincidence.

\begin{proposition}[Surjective Function Composition is Surjective]\label{prop:composition-of-surjective-functions-is-surjective}
    Let $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ be sets. Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: C \to D$ be surjections such that $g \circ f$ is defined. Then $g \circ f$ is a surjection.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    See \myref{exercise:composition-of-surjective-functions-is-surjective} (later).
\end{proof}

The overall effect of \myref{prop:composition-of-injective-functions-is-injective} and \myref{prop:composition-of-surjective-functions-is-surjective} is the following.

\begin{proposition}[Bijective Function Composition is Bijective]
    Let $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ be sets. Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: C \to D$ be bijections such that $g \circ f$ is defined. Then $g \circ f$ is a bijection.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Since $f$ and $g$ are bijections, they are both injective and surjective. \myref{prop:composition-of-injective-functions-is-injective} thus tells us that $g \circ f$ is injective and \myref{prop:composition-of-surjective-functions-is-surjective} tells us that $g \circ f$ is surjective, which means that $g \circ f$ is bijective.
\end{proof}

\begin{exercise}[Composition of Surjective Functions is Surjective]\label{exercise:composition-of-surjective-functions-is-surjective}
    Prove \myref{prop:composition-of-surjective-functions-is-surjective}.
\end{exercise}
