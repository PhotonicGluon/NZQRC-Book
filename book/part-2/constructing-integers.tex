\Chapter{Defining the Integers}
\Lettrine{Constructing} more general numbers requires the numbers we defined before; we will use natural numbers and equivalence relations to create a definition for the integers. We will also define relationships between integers, just like we did with the natural numbers.

\section{The Insufficiency of the Natural Numbers}
Consider the action of adding two natural numbers, say 2 and 3. We know that $3 + 4 = 7$ by definition, but how do we express this diagrammatically?

Let us consider a \term{number line}\index{number line}. We place the natural numbers on the number line, and draw a line between each pair of adjacent numbers.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=8cm]{images/part-2/constructing-integers/number-line_raw.png}
    \caption{The Number Line}
\end{figure}
When we add 4 to 3, we first move right by 3 (see the first arrow below) and then move along by 4 afterwards (see the second arrow below).
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=8cm]{images/part-2/constructing-integers/number-line_add-3-then-4.png}
    \caption{Adding 4 to 3}
\end{figure}
Of course, the result is the same if we first move right by 4 and then move along by 3, due to commutativity of addition (\myref{theorem:addition-commutative-naturals}).
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=8cm]{images/part-2/constructing-integers/number-line_add-4-then-3.png}
    \caption{Adding 3 to 4}
\end{figure}

What if there was a way to ``undo'' addition? Suppose we have the result of 7, and we knew that one of the numbers is 3. What is the other number? Diagrammatically, this looks like moving left by 3 from 7.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=8cm]{images/part-2/constructing-integers/number-line_sub-3-from-7.png}
    \caption{Undoing Adding 3 From 7}
\end{figure}

For some numbers, we are above to find a natural number if we perform this ``undo'' operation. For others, we can't. For example, what if we move left by 4 from 1? There simply is no natural number that is there.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=6cm]{images/part-2/constructing-integers/number-line_sub-3-from-1.png}
    \caption{Undoing Adding 3 From 1}
\end{figure}

This is the insufficiency of the natural numbers that we wish to address by defining the integers -- a way to move an arbitrary number of units left or right on the number line.

\section{Leftward and Rightward ``Shifts'' of Natural Numbers}
Let us use these shifts to define numbers. The natural numbers can easily be defined this way -- for example, the natural number 4 can be thought of as the number obtained by performing a rightward shift of 7 followed by a leftward shift of 3.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=8cm]{images/part-2/constructing-integers/lr-shifts_4-shifts-1.png}
    \caption{Creating 4 By Using Leftward and Rightward ``Shifts''}
\end{figure}
We may denote this pair of shifts by $(7, 3)$, signifying that we shift rightward by 7 and then shift leftward by 3. Of course, this is not the only combination of shifts that result in 4. We could have, for instance, shifted rightward by 5 and then leftward by 1, or shifted rightward by 6 and then leftward by 2, or shifted rightward by 8 and then leftward by 4, and so on.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=8cm]{images/part-2/constructing-integers/lr-shifts_4-shifts-2.png}
    \caption{Other Ways to Create 4}
\end{figure}
But clearly all these shifts just result in 4! We want a way to say that these different combinations of shifts are the same. To put it more concretely, we want an equivalence relation $\EqRel$ on the set $\N^2$ such that $(a,b) \EqRel (c,d)$ if and only if a rightward shift of $a$ followed by a leftward shift of $b$ is the same as a rightward shift of $c$ followed by a leftward shift of $d$, for all $a,b,c,d \in \N$. That way, we can say that $(5, 1) \EqRel (6, 2)$, $(6, 2) \EqRel (7, 3)$, $(7, 3) \EqRel (8, 4)$, and so on.

\begin{exercise}[Defining the ``Shift Equivalence'' Relation]
    Create a definition for $\EqRel$ as mentioned above, proving that it is an equivalence relation.\newline
    (\textit{Note: you are restricted to using the operations defined in \myref{chapter:operations-on-naturals}}.)
\end{exercise}

Of course, there's nothing stopping us from creating a resultant shift that is not within the natural numbers. Consider the shift defined by $(2, 5)$. This means that we first shift rightward by 2, and then shift leftwards by 5. But clearly the result (marked by the question mark) is not a natural number, and yet it does not matter.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=8cm]{images/part-2/constructing-integers/lr-shifts_-3-shifts-1.png}
    \caption{A Non-Natural Number Combination of ``Shifts''}
\end{figure}
Again, there are other combinations of shifts that result in `$?$', and they are all equivalent via $\EqRel$. For example, one sees $(1, 4) \EqRel (2, 5)$, $(2, 5) \EqRel (3, 6)$, $(3, 6) \EqRel (4, 7)$, and so on.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=8cm]{images/part-2/constructing-integers/lr-shifts_-3-shifts-2.png}
    \caption{Other Ways to Create `$?$'}
\end{figure}

With the equivalence relation $\EqRel$, we are ready to define the integers.

\section{The Set of Integers}
Notice that the shifts of $(5, 1)$, $(6, 2)$, $(7, 3)$, and $(8, 4)$ all represent the natural number 4. We can thus think of all these shifts as the same thing by considering their equivalence class under $\EqRel$. Since they are all related to each other, thus $[(5, 1)] = [(6, 2)] = [(7, 3)] = [(8, 4)] = \cdots$ (\myref{theorem:equivalence-class-equivalence}). One notices that $[(4, 0)] = [(5, 1)]$, and it is precisely the natural number 4 that the shifts in the equivalence class $[(4, 0)]$ represent. Thus, we can define the integer 4 to be $[(4, 0)]$. We can do this for the rest of the natural numbers.

\begin{definition}
    Let $n$ be a natural number. The integer $n$ is the equivalence class $[(n, 0)]$.
\end{definition}

What about the shifts $(1,4)$, $(2,5)$, $(3,6)$, $(4,7)$, and so on? These are not equivalent to any natural number, but are numbers found on the `left' of 0. In particular, these shifts all result in an overall leftward shift of 3, so they all belong in the equivalence class $[(0, 3)]$. We can define an integer to represent this equivalence class.

\begin{definition}
    Let $n$ be a non-zero natural number. The integer $-n$, read as ``negative $n$'', is the equivalence class $[(0, n)]$.
\end{definition}

We can visualize these equivalence classes on a two-dimensional grid. A shift $(m,n)$ is represented by a point that is $m$ along the horizontal axis and $n$ along the vertical axis, and shifts belonging in the same equivalence class lie on the same dotted line.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[height=4.5cm]{images/part-2/constructing-integers/integers_number-grid.png}
    \caption{Integers as Equivalence Classes}
\end{figure}

With these definitions out of the way, we are now ready to define the set of integers.
\begin{definition}[The Set of Integers]
    The \term{set of integers}\index{integer!set}, denoted $\Z$, is the set of equivalence classes under $\EqRel$. That is,
    \[
        \Z = \{[(m, n)] \vert m, n \in \N\}
    \]
    where $[(m,n)]$ is the equivalence class of $(m,n)$ under $\EqRel$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}[$\N$ as a Subset of $\Z$]
    One notes that the equivalence classes are not exactly natural numbers. However, we will think of the natural numbers as being `embedded' in the integers. This means that when we write $\N \subseteq \Z$, we think of the representative equivalence classes of the natural numbers. More concretely, think about $\N$ within $\Z$ is that $\N$, in this context, is the range of the function $\EmbedFunc{\N}: \N \to \Z$ where
    \[
        \EmbedFunc{\N}(n) = [(n, 0)].
    \]
\end{remark}

Let us now define the negation of an integer.
\begin{definition}[Negation]
    The \term{negation operation}\index{negation} is a unary operation given by the function $\mathrm{neg}: \Z \to \Z$ such that
    \[
        \mathrm{neg}([(m, n)]) = [(n, m)].
    \]
    We denote the negation of an integer $x$ by $-x$. That is, for any integer $x = [(m, n)]$, where $m, n \in \N$, we have $-x = [(n, m)]$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
        \item $1 = [(1, 0)]$ and $-1 = [(0, 1)]$.
        \item $5 = [(5, 0)]$ and $-5 = [(0, 5)]$.
        \item $0 = [(0, 0)]$ and $-0 = [(0, 0)]$, which is the same as 0.
        \item $-3 = [(0, 3)]$ and $-(-3) = [(3, 0)]$, which is the same as 3.
    \end{itemize}
\end{example}

The observation that $-(-3)$ is the same as 3 leads us to define a more general result.
\begin{proposition}[Negation of Negative]
    $-(-x) = x$ for all $x \in \Z$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Suppose $x = [(m, n)]$ where $m, n \in \N$. Note that $-x = [(n, m)]$ by definition of negation, and so $-(-x) = [(m, n)] = x$.
\end{proof}

With negation defined, we can define the absolute value operation.
\begin{definition}[Absolute Value]
    The \term{absolute value operation}\index{absolute value} is a unary operation given by the function $\abs: \Z \to \Z$ such that
    \[
        \abs([(m, n)]) = \begin{cases}
            [(m, n)] & \text{if } n \leq m\\
            [(n, m)] & \text{if } m < n
        \end{cases}
    \]
    We denote the absolute value of an integer $x$ by $|x|$. That is, for any integer $x$ we have
    \[
        |x| = \begin{cases}
            x & \text{if } x \in \N\\
            -x & \text{if } x \notin \N
        \end{cases}
    \]
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}[$|\cdot|$ As Cardinality Versus $|\cdot|$ As Absolute Value]
    Recall that $|A|$ denotes the cardinality of a set $A$, which can be confusing when $|n|$ denotes the absolute value of an integer $n$. If there is any chance of confusion, we will use $\abs(n)$ to denote the absolute value of an integer instead of $|n|$.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
        \item $|1| = \abs([(1, 0)]) = [(1, 0)] = 1$.
        \item $|0| = \abs([(0, 0)]) = [(0, 0)] = 0$.
        \item $|-3| = \abs([(0, 3)]) = [(3, 0)] = 3$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{example}

\section{Ordering of Integers}
Earlier, we defined the ``less than or equal to'' relation ($\leq$) on the natural numbers, which is a total order on the naturals (\myref{prop:leq-is-total-order}) and thus gives us a way to order the natural numbers. We can do the same for the integers.

\begin{definition}[Less Than or Equal To Relation (Integers)]
    The \term{less than or equal to relation}\index{integer!less than or equal to} on the integers is the relation $\leq$ on $\Z$ where
    \[
        [(a,b)] \leq [(c,d)] \iff a + d \leq b + c
    \]
    for any $[(a,b)], [(c,d)] \in \Z$.
\end{definition}
Note that the ``$\leq$'' on the right-hand side of the definition is the ``less than or equal to'' partial order on the natural numbers.

We can similarly define the ``less than'' relation on the integers.

\begin{definition}[Less Than Relation (Integers)]
    The \term{less than relation}\index{integer!less than} on the integers is the relation $<$ on $\Z$ where
    \[
        [(a,b)] < [(c,d)] \iff a + d < b + c
    \]
    for any $[(a,b)], [(c,d)] \in \Z$.
\end{definition}

\begin{exercise}[Proving Partial Orders]
    Prove that
    \begin{partquestions}{\alph*}
        \item $\leq$ on $\Z$ is a partial order; and
        \item $<$ on $\Z$ is a strict partial order.
    \end{partquestions}
\end{exercise}

Using \myref{prop:por-inverse-is-also-por} we can also define the ``greater than or equal to'' partial order relation and ``greater than'' strict partial order relation on the integers.

\begin{definition}[Greater Than or Equal To Relation (Integers)]
    The \term{greater than or equal relation}\index{integer!greater than} on the integers is the relation $\geq$ on $\Z$ where $\geq = \leq^{-1}$, i.e. $m \geq n$ if and only if $n \leq m$ for any $m, n \in \Z$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Greater Than Relation (Integers)]
    The \term{greater than relation}\index{integer!greater than} on the integers is the relation $<$ on $\Z$ where $< = >^{-1}$, i.e. $m > n$ if and only if $n < m$ for any $m, n \in \Z$.
\end{definition}

It should come as no surprise that, like with the natural numbers, the ``less than or equal to'' relation ($\leq$) on the integers is a total order.

\begin{proposition}[$\leq$ is a Total Order on $\Z$]
    $\leq$ on $\Z$ is a total order.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    We are to show that, for any $m, n \in \Z$, either $m \leq n$ or $n \leq m$.

    Let $m, n \in \Z$. Write
    \begin{align*}
        m &= [(a_m, b_m)]\\
        n &= [(a_n, b_n)]
    \end{align*}
    where $a_m, b_m, a_n, b_n \in \N$. Note that $a_m + b_n \in \N$ and $b_m + a_n \in \N$. Since $\leq$ on $\N$ is a total order, thus we must have either $a_m + b_n \leq b_m + a_n$ or $b_m + a_n \leq a_m + b_n$. The first case leads to $[(a_m, b_m)] \leq [(a_n, b_n)]$ and the second leads to $[(a_n, b_n)] \leq [(a_m, b_m)]$, i.e. either $m \leq n$ or $n \leq m$. This proves that $\leq$ on $\Z$ is a total order.
\end{proof}

We can draw a Hasse diagram for $\leq$ on $\Z$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[height=4.5cm]{images/part-2/constructing-integers/integer-ordering.png}
    \caption{$\leq$ is a Total Order on $\Z$}  
\end{figure}

One clearly sees that $\Z$ has no maximal, minimal, greatest, and least element.
